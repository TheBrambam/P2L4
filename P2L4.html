How Level 4 lighting works

Every face of the shared cube has an outward-pointing normal vector (±X, ±Y or ±Z). The vertex shader transforms that normal with the cube's rotation and inverse scale, so lighting works even when parts are rotated or stretched. The fragment shader implements ambient + Lambert diffuse + Blinn–Phong specular lighting. Ambient light makes shadow-facing sides visible, Lambert diffuse uses max(dot(N,L), 0) to brighten faces oriented toward the sun, and specular highlights use the half-vector between sun and camera directions. Sun azimuth and elevation control the world-space light direction. Disabling lighting shows the original vertex colors.

The light is directional (like distant sunlight), so all cube objects share one direction; this is a lighting calculation, not shadow mapping. Cubes do not cast shadows onto one another. The sliders update every frame without rebuilding the terrain.

How the cubic Bézier sky paths work

A moving cloud, a blimp, and two birds follow cubic Bézier curves using four 3D control points: P₀ is the start, P₁ and P₂ bend the route, and P₃ is the end. At normalized time t between 0 and 1, the position is B(t) = (1−t)³P₀ + 3(1−t)²tP₁ + 3(1−t)t²P₂ + t³P₃. The code computes the X, Y, and Z coordinates separately, and uses the curve derivative to rotate the blimp and birds along their travel direction.

Each object has its own duration and phase offset. When its travel time reaches the duration, the modulo operator (%) resets t to zero; it reappears at P₀ and repeats the journey. Path endpoints are deliberately outside the main grid, so the return normally happens beyond the visible landscape. The floating island and two other clouds remain stationary.
